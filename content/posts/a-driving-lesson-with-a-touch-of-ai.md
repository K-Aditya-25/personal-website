I've held an Indian driving licence for 5 years now. However, as per the law, I've had to take driving lessons in-order to get an Irish driving licence.

In this blog, I tell you an interesting story of how my first driving lesson in Ireland surprisingly turned into a conversation around AI.

## TLDR

My first driving lesson in Ireland - after having driven for 5 years in India. We talked about whether the AI hype is real, what jobs are at the highest risk because of AI, whether self-driving Dublin buses make sense, and my two bits on how to survive in this day and age of AI.

# How Did The Conversation Take Place?

My instructor was a huge guy. An Irish man in his late twenties to early thirties, he was probably 1.5 times my size.

At 9 am on a Friday morning, he was waiting for me in his red Ford right outside my accommodation.

As I started driving, it took me about 20 mins to convince him that I know how to drive it.

30 mins after that, he felt that he had told me almost everything that I needed to know for that lesson.

Almost an hour into it, I get asked:

> So, what do you think of AI?

# What Did We Talk About?

For the next hour, it felt like we were in a podcast talking about AI, the future of jobs, and the future of public transport in Dublin.

In this blog, I want to talk about the conversations that I enjoyed the most and that stuck with me after the lesson.

## Is AI all hype or is it actually useful?

These are such interesting times. AI is clearly a bubble, albeit an expanding one.

Today, if you're not working in the tech sector, I'd assume that you rarely use AI for work. Majority of the human workforce today cannot care less about Agentic AI, RAG, AI skills, MCPs, and fancy terms like context engineering, harness engineering, prompt loops or graphs.

So, when my driving instructor asked this question, I totally understood where he was coming from. As I navigated my way around traffic signals, lanes and speed limits, I got to thinking and here's my take on this.

Where you work and what you do on a day-day basis often plays a major part in how you see AI. Let's break it down.

![](/images/blog/a-driving-lesson-with-a-touch-of-ai/image-01.png)

Say you're someone who deals with physical objects or humans quite a lot. You could be an electrician, a plumber, a soldier, a farmer, a therapist, a cook, a mechanic, a lawyer and so much more. To you, AI might seem as simple as google with emotional support. Something that can search information like Google, and also talk to you the way you want it when you need it.

Although not impossible, it is rare to see someone who isn't working in a tech company (or academia) utilise AI like the people in the bubble do. So, for them, AI could be perceived as more hype than useful.

However, if you work in a tech company or, your nature of work involves dealing with / talking about tech companies or, you're in academia, there is a higher chance that you find AI to be useful.

Tech companies have invested billions, if not trillions into AI leading us into the AI boom, hoping for the demand to one day reach to a level that meets their expectations from the future. *(*[*read Bloomberg's piece on circular financing in AI*](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-ai-circular-deals/))

Hence, there's naturally a much higher chance for people working within the tech sector to see AI as useful more than hype. Their use cases might be as simple as getting AI to organise their inbox to something as complex as synthesising new proteins to cure diseases that are currently incurable.

My point being, there is no simple answer to whether today's state of AI is all hype or real. There is nuance to it. And the nature of your work and your awareness of AI will likely influence your opinion on this.

Personally, I believe that AI is, and can be, very useful only if you know how to filter out the fluff from the internet to apply it to real personal and business use cases.

Don't get me wrong, the AI hype-train is very real. Everyday I see people jumping on the new trend in the market. Recently, people have been jumping from loop engineering to graph engineering to harness engineering and now decision models are the new talk of the town.

What matters is how capable you are to extract real value from all the hype in the internet around AI. Fortunately, I've been able to do exactly that.

## What set of jobs are at the highest risk because of AI?

After learning that I'm not supposed to drive on hash lines and about the significance of filter lights on the road, my driving instructor started making more small talk.

*So, what job would you be doing?* , he asked. *A junior technical business analyst at Web Summit, the Irish-led tech conference,* I said. Although, I like to think of myself as more of an AI engineer :)

Naturally, he followed up with.

> What jobs would be at the highest risk because of AI?

He had instructed another lad from India who was working at Amazon a couple months ago. The guy must've told him how he's concerned with all the layoffs that Amazon has been doing.

My driving instructor was keen to know my take on it.

Lucky for me - I had read Anthropic's piece on [**Labor market impacts of AI**](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts?e45d281a_page=4&939688b5_page=2&et_core_page_resource=&et_blog=)**.** It is a good starting point to answer this sort of a question.

In their blog, Anthropic put an interesting diagram comparing the theoretical coverage of AI across different fields of work versus their observed usage of AI (specifically Claude) in those fields.

![](/images/blog/a-driving-lesson-with-a-touch-of-ai/image-02.png)

To break this diagram down, the researchers at Anthropic believe that AI could theoretically automate majority of the tasks of professions involved in Management, Business & Finance, Office & Admin etc (essentially - desk jobs). The theoretical coverage goes as far as above 90% in professions involved heavily with computer and math. This is obvious since LLMs today are quite good at math and code.

Computer and Math professionals had the highest observed coverage (~33%) indicating that Claude is able to easily automate up-to 33% of the their tasks in general. However, comparing with the theoretical coverage of ~90%, Anthropic believes that there is still a lot of room for improving the capabilities of Claude, i.e., scary/exciting times ahead for software engineers (depends on how you look at it).

On the flip side, jobs like construction, installation and repair, maintenance, transportation etc have the lowest theoretical coverage of AI and an even lower observed coverage.

Hence, the following table (also from Anthropic's piece on the labor market) that demonstrates the occupations that are the most exposed to AI will make sense to you.

![](/images/blog/a-driving-lesson-with-a-touch-of-ai/image-03.png)

The above table shows that computer programmers, customer service representatives and data entry keyers are the top 3 professions that are most exposed to AI - which makes sense given how good AI is becoming at code and doing simple tasks like manual data entry.

On the other hand, the article also mentions that professions like Cooks, Motorcycle Mechanics, Lifeguards, Bartenders, Dishwashers, and Dressing Room Attendants have the least zero AI coverage, and subsequently, the least exposed to AI - which also makes sense since their tasks primarily involve physical work and dealing with people.

So, my answer was heavily influenced from this article. I also believe that desk jobs are at the highest risk because of AI.

Given that tech companies have made huge investments into AI and they are far from seeing any meaningful returns on those investments, I believe that layoffs at tech jobs are only going to increase.

## Could Dublin Bus be self driven one day?

This one was a funny one. If you've lived in Dublin, you must know that the Dublin Bus is the lifeline of its public transport network. I would even go as far to say that the Dublin Bus is the lifeline of city life in Dublin!

![](/images/blog/a-driving-lesson-with-a-touch-of-ai/image-04.png)

There's a popular joke around here that there isn't a metro that connects the Dublin Airport to the Dublin City Centre, however, you could take the No. 16 bus :)

This conversation started when we were discussing the ways in which AI is being used in daily life today. I went over how AI (deep learning models) is extensively used in self-driving cars today (Tesla, Waymo etc).

Naturally, the driving instructor asked.

> Could Dublin Bus be self-driven one day?

My answer - **No**. Three reasons why I think so.

1.  Dublin Bus uses the same road network as all other vehicles in the city. Full Self Driving (FSD) for cars has a long way to go in Dublin, I cannot see a future where FSD for Dublin buses will be solved anytime soon.
    
2.  Regulations in EU are tricky to get through. They can be especially tricky in this case because:
    
    1.  FSD buses will be putting thousands of drivers out of a job.
        
    2.  With FSD, there's a constant problem of identifying the responsible party in case of an accident.
        
    3.  The job of a bus driver is as much people-oriented as it is of a driver. There are so many decisions that the driver makes on the fly - whether to let a person in who is clearly in need, managing any nuisance on the bus etc. I don't see a time when people would want an AI to be in charge of that.
        
3.  Buses aren't trains/metros. Trains have been automated in quite a lot of places. The Delhi metro's Pink and Magenta lines operate fully driverless! And that has been possible partly because
    
    1.  Trains travel mostly in straight lines or slight curves.
        
    2.  Trains have a dedicated track. So the problem of sharing the track with other modes of transport doesn't exist.
        

## How to survive in the day and age of AI?

Our conversation ended after I was nearing my accommodation. This was the last question.

> How do you survive today with the likes of AI?

Well, there's no easy answer to this question. For every answer to this question, I am sure there must be hundreds of stories out there suggesting otherwise. However, for the sake of the conversation, here are my two bits on this.

I believe that although AI is yet to reach its full potential, it is quite capable as it is. So capable, that it has already started to replace jobs and changing expectations. As Anthropic's research on Labor market impacts of AI shows, the desk-jobs are the ones which have been hit the hardest.

So yes, it is becoming increasingly difficult to hold onto your job (especially if you work in a tech company and/or a desk job in general) with the rapid developments in AI. This is how I think one could survive, or even, thrive.

### Constant Up-Skilling

The times where you could just coast in a company by doing the bare minimum are gone. Earlier, doing the bare minimum didn't guarantee that you could thrive in an organisation, however, in most cases, you'd still have your job.

Now, you need to constantly be up-skilling to give yourself a better chance at surviving the layoffs, or finding a new/better job.

Constantly working on expanding your knowledge base is hard. It is certainly not everybody's cup of tea. However, that's exactly what gives you the best chance to thrive in today's world with AI.

Currently, I am following along [Stanford's Engineering AI Agents](https://cs329z.stanford.edu/) course to learn about how to make AI agents from the ground up. I am on constant look-out for the next good course/book/article I can take or read. This helps me stay on the forefront of the new developments in AI.

### Embracing AI

Learn to embrace AI. Working with AI is going to be the new norm regardless of how you like it. You're only going to increase your chances of survival by learning how to best incorporate it in your daily work-life.

In my job, I am always thinking about how I can best utilise the AI usage given to us. At the moment, I barely hit 35% of my weekly claude limits.

Not to say that I aim to burn the tokens recklessly, but I still believe there's so much more value I can bring to the organisation just by using Claude, something that the company has already paid for, to the best of my ability.

Enterprises are spending money left and right on AI. While I won't comment on whether that's the right thing to do, as an employee, the best thing you can do is make that investment worth it. Generate value with AI wherever you can.

### Becoming more AI Literate

Start educating yourself on AI. It may seem overwhelming at first, but there are tons of resources out there that handhold you on learning how AI works and how to work with AI.

I see it this way - the more AI literate I become, the more chance I am giving myself and my organisation of finding new/better solutions to current problems.

I regularly read articles, blogs and tweets of people and organisations who publish their results and experiences with new AI projects. This way I make myself more AI literate and get constant inspiration on how I can solve problems at my organisation.
